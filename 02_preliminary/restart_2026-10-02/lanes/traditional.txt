Traditional lane — evidence brief (cutoff 2026-10-02)

Main findings

1. The “open window” is not one uniform drop in immunity. Following acute exercise, circulating leukocytes and lymphocytes may rise during or immediately after the bout, then some subsets fall below baseline during recovery; cell counts and measured function follow different trajectories. In a meta-analysis of 12 studies, NK cytolytic activity increased immediately after exercise and was not below pre-exercise values during the first two recovery hours. Its change was not explained by NK-cell count. A small 10-cyclist study after two hours at 90% of ventilatory threshold found lower circulating CD16+/56+ NK-cell counts through 8 h and reduced neutrophil phagocytosis at 2–24 h, while neutrophil oxidative burst did not change. These outcomes establish marker-specific kinetics; they do not establish a clinically open infection window. [Rumpf 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33398798/), [Kakanis 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20839496/)

2. The 30-minute exclusion rule should be removed. Immediate/0–15-minute samples reveal the initial mobilization and are necessary to interpret later lymphopenia or subset redistribution. A 2026 meta-analysis of 21 SIT studies (359 participants) found acute increases in total leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and IL-6; sIgA was stable among trained athletes. That analysis emphasized the first post-exercise hour and did not test infection risk or later recovery. Record sample time in phases instead of excluding early observations. [Liang et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41705158/), [Kakanis et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20839496/)

3. sIgA concentration is not interchangeable with secretion rate or protein-normalized IgA. In 98 marathon runners, 1.5 h after the race sIgA concentration, IgA/protein ratio, and secretion rate fell by 21%, 31%, and 25%; among the 16 runners later reporting URTI, only the IgA/protein ratio differed, while concentration and secretion rate did not. A later 14-swimmer seasonal study found lower absolute sIgA concentration during symptom weeks but no association for secretion rate or saliva flow; its proposed individual pre-symptom trend was not statistically significant. In a 2025 two-part study, exercise-related mucosal immune response was not associated with post-marathon symptom-defined RTI risk. [Nieman et al. 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11774070/), [Baker et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38071136/), [Harrison et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12480926/)

4. There is a small, historically important prospective signal, but not validated individual prediction. A seven-month cohort of 26 elite swimmers plus 12 controls reported physician-verified infections; preseason and repeated pretraining sIgA values/slopes predicted infection counts in within-cohort regressions. Later cohorts are smaller, have symptom-based outcomes, and do not consistently replicate the saliva finding. No retrieved conventional-marker study prospectively linked acute post-exercise biomarker changes to independently confirmed pathogen infection with external prediction validation. [Gleeson et al. 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9927012/), [Baker et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38071136/), [Harrison et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12480926/)

5. Endpoint labels need to stay precise. The 2024 swimmer and 2025 marathon cohorts used self-reported respiratory symptoms; the 2018 marathon study applied a clinical LRTI syndrome definition that included fever and symptom duration but did no pathogen testing; the 1999 swimmer cohort reports physician verification without describing pathogen assays. Davison et al. recommend validated illness logs, broad pathogen screening, in vivo/integrated measures, adequate sample size, and stronger control of exposure, sleep, nutrition, stress, and related confounders. [Baker et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38071136/), [Harrison et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12480926/), [Cantó et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0206059), [Davison et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40709812/)

Closest and most useful evidence

- Closest positive predictive result: Gleeson et al. (1999), a seven-month swimmer cohort with physician-verified infections and preseason/pretraining sIgA regression associations. It is small, has no independent validation, and does not establish pathogen-confirmed post-bout infection.
- Best recent prospective athlete monitor: Baker et al. (2024), weekly sIgA and symptom/training-load tracking over 8 months in 14 elite swimmers. It finds symptom/high-load associations but also shows concentration, secretion rate, and flow do not tell the same story.
- Most useful recent null link between a post-exercise traditional marker and future clinical symptoms: Harrison et al. (2025), 406 marathon participants plus a controlled 45-person exercise/rest study. Self-reported RTI was associated with anxiety/mood measures; SIgA response itself was not associated with RTI risk.
- Best method critique to anchor endpoint decisions: Davison et al. (2025). It explicitly argues for illness outcomes, pathogen screening, in vivo function, sample-size adequacy, and control of exposure/confounding.

Evidence on both sides of “open window”

Evidence consistent with transient perturbation includes post-marathon reductions in salivary IgA measures, small post-exercise cohorts reporting later NK-subset or neutrophil-function changes, and seasonal sIgA/illness associations. The strongest modern acute NK meta-analysis, however, finds NK cytolytic activity elevated immediately and not suppressed below baseline in the first two recovery hours; its change is independent of NK-cell count. Campbell and Turner (2018) argue that post-exercise lymphopenia is largely redistribution and that existing infection evidence does not substantiate global immunosuppression. The fair conclusion is that acute marker shifts are real and workload/timing-specific, while a causal, clinically predictive 3–72-hour infection-susceptibility interval remains unproven by these traditional-marker studies. [Rumpf et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33398798/), [Campbell & Turner 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5911985/), [Kakanis et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20839496/)

Protocol changes suggested by this lane

- Replace the exclusion of post-exercise follow-up under 30 minutes with timing bins: during exercise, immediate/0–<30 min, 30–120 min, 2–8 h, 8–24 h, 24–72 h, and later. Keep acute, repeated-training, competition, and seasonal cohort studies in separate design strata.
- Separate four evidence claims in charting: marker changes; measured immune function; association with symptoms/clinical illness/infection; and prospective prediction. Do not infer infection causation or prediction from ex vivo marker changes.
- Grade respiratory outcomes as pathogen-confirmed, clinician/clinical-syndrome defined, standardized symptom scale, or self-reported symptoms; record the case definition, blinded adjudication, test panel, swab timing, and follow-up.
- For NK, record absolute counts, subsets, percentages, cell-normalized function, assay type, effector-to-target ratio, target cell, stimulation, and sampling time separately.
- For sIgA, extract concentration, collection method/time, saliva flow, secretion rate, total-protein normalization, units, and individual versus group-level change. Do not collapse concentration and secretion rate.
- For white-cell, cytokine, and cortisol measures, record absolute versus relative counts, matrix, blood-volume correction, assay, time of day, exercise dose, training status, nutrition, sleep, stress, and pathogen exposure.
- Require model timing and validation fields for prediction claims: prospective ordering, prespecified threshold, discrimination, calibration, internal/external validation.

Evidence gaps

The central gap is a sufficiently sized healthy-person cohort with dense sampling across the acute recovery timeline and broad pathogen testing of incident illness. Current traditional-marker studies mostly measure symptoms rather than microbiologically confirmed infection, and historical within-cohort predictions have not become validated tools. Sex, training status, exposure, season, oral hydration, psychological stress, and assay normalization need better control.
